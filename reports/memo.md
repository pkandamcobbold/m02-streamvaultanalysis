# Back-catalog licensing: what to buy

**Buy franchise bundles first.** Across every way we measured it, films that
come as part of a series earn back more per dollar spent than one-off titles,
and they do it more reliably — and that holds whether you look at the whole
catalog or only at films from 2000 onward.

**Why.** A typical series film earned back 5.5 times what it cost to make,
against 5.0 times for a typical one-off (87 series films and 25 one-offs). The
weaker end matters more than the average here: the bottom quarter of series
films still returned 3.9 times, where the bottom quarter of one-offs managed
2.6 times. And 95% of series films cleared our break-even bar against 80% of
one-offs.

One caution on that comparison, because it will be raised: if you compare
*averages* instead of typical films, one-offs come out ahead. That is the work
of three exceptional films from the 1960s, 70s and 90s — nothing on offer
today behaves like them, and an average that leans on them is not a guide to a
purchase. The figures above describe the middle of each group.

## The two views, side by side

| | Series films | One-off films |
|---|---|---|
| **Whole catalog** — how many | 87 | 25 |
| typical return | 5.5 times | 5.0 times |
| weaker end | 3.9 times | 2.6 times |
| cleared break-even | 95% | 80% |
| **2000 onward** — how many | 59 | 12 |
| typical return | 5.1 times | 4.5 times |
| weaker end | 3.8 times | 2.9 times |
| cleared break-even | 95% | 92% |

**One thing does change after 2000.** Across the whole catalog, series films
are not just better on average but far more *predictable* — their results
cluster tightly where one-offs are all over the place. Among films from 2000
onward that advantage disappears: the two groups are equally unpredictable.
The margin is small and rests on only 12 one-off films, so we would not say
one-offs have become *safer* — but we would stop arguing that recent series
films are the steadier bet. If anyone in the room makes that argument, it is
not supported.

## The deal note

**Star Wars Collection** is the strongest thing on the list, and it is strong
for a reason worth knowing before you negotiate: its value is spread across
the whole set, not carried by one title. Its biggest earner is 24% of the
set's total box office, which is the *lowest* concentration of any series we
looked at. Compare a set where one film is half the value: there you are
paying bundle prices for one title and some filler.

**What that means for price.** We have no licensing quotes, so we cannot tell
you what to pay — anyone who gives you a dollar figure from this data is
guessing. What we can say is *relative*: this set justifies a higher price per
film than the others on the list, and the others justify a higher price than
the one-off titles.

**How much higher.** This set's typical film returns about 158% of what a
typical film in the next-best sets returns — so a price premium per film of
roughly that order is defensible, and much beyond it is not supported by
anything we measured. Treat that as the shape of the gap, not a ceiling: it is
a ratio of past performance, not of licensing value.

**On later instalments.** There is a real pattern: the first film in a series
returns more than the last. But it is much weaker than it first appears — most
of the apparent gap is that opening films in this list are simply older, and
older films look more profitable for reasons that have nothing to do with
quality. Comparing like with like, a final instalment returns roughly a
seventh less than an opener, not the half the raw figures suggest. Useful at
the table as a discount argument; not a reason to refuse a set. And the team's
specific belief that the *middle* films are the weak ones is not supported —
the middle films hold up; it is the last ones that slip.

## What we could not stand behind

Ordered by how much it should change your confidence.

1. **We cannot tell you how these films behave on a streaming service.** What
   we have is cinema box office and public audience ratings. Those are a
   stand-in for streaming value, not a measure of it. Every number here is
   about how a film performed in cinemas, sometimes decades ago.
2. **Nothing on this list ever lost money.** The worst performer on the whole
   candidate list still made back more than it cost. That means we cannot test
   the argument that series films protect you on the downside — there is no
   downside in this data to compare. If that argument matters to the board,
   it is unproven either way, and this list cannot settle it.
3. **The one-off group is thin.** 25 films across the whole catalog, and 12 from 2000 onward. Those figures move noticeably if a single film is added or
   removed. The direction of our recommendation is solid; the size of the gap
   is not precise.
4. **Old and new money are not the same money.** Budgets and box office are in
   the dollars of the year each film was released, and an older film's takings
   keep accruing through re-releases while its cost stays fixed. Older films
   therefore look more profitable than they were. This is why we report the two
   views separately and never compare a figure across them.
5. **The candidate list was already filtered toward series films** before it
   reached us — nearly four in five candidates belong to one. So this analysis
   tells you which of *these* titles to buy. It cannot tell you that series
   films are more available in the market generally.
6. **We have no licensing prices.** The whole analysis is about value, not
   cost, so it can rank what to pursue and cannot tell you what to pay.

## Where the titles went

**127 titles received → 112 analysed → 15 set aside**, for these reasons:

| Titles | What happened |
|---|---|
| 112 | analysed |
| 6 | the same title listed twice — counted once |
| 5 | no longer exists in the public film database |
| 3 | no usable reference number (a placeholder or a blank row) |
| 1 | the same title again, written in a different numbering system — counted once |

Of the titles set aside, most are not losses: they are the same films listed
more than once, and removing the duplicates prevents counting them twice. The
genuine losses are the handful with no usable reference and the few that no
longer exist in the database — and on the evidence those were probably never
back-catalog films in the first place.

**The figures in this memo describe the data as it stood on 2026-11-19.** They
do not update on their own. Re-running the analysis refreshes both the figures
and this date.
