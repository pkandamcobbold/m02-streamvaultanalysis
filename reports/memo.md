## What I found

**127 titles received, 112 analysed, 15 set aside** — mostly the same films
listed twice. Eight rows are genuine losses. The full account is in the
attached review.

| | Franchise | Standalone | Franchise 2000+ | Standalone 2000+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How many films | 87 | 25 | 59 | 12 |
| Typical return | 5.5 times | 5.0 times | 5.1 times | 4.5 times |
| Weaker end | 3.9 times | 2.6 times | 3.8 times | 2.9 times |
| Cleared break-even | 95% | 80% | 95% | 92% |

Franchise films lead on all three measures, in both windows. The weaker end
matters most for a purchase like this.

**How solid is that lead?** Resampling the list, the gap on typical return
could plausibly sit anywhere from -6.2 to +3.2 times back, and once films in
the same franchise count as one bet rather than several, no single measure's
lead is large enough for this list to establish on its own. What does hold is
the agreement: franchise films come out ahead on all four measures in 78% to
97% of resamples. The recommendation rests on that, not on the half-turn gap.

**One caution, because it will be raised:** if you compare *averages* instead
of typical films, standalones come out ahead — the work of three exceptional
films from the 1960s, 70s and 90s, and nothing on offer today behaves like
them.

**One thing changes after 2000.** Franchise films are far more *predictable*
across the whole catalog; among recent films that advantage disappears. The
margin is small and rests on only 12 standalone films, so we would not say
standalones have become *safer* — but if anyone argues recent franchise films
are the steadier bet, it is not supported.

**Later instalments are not worth less — once you compare franchises of the
same age.** The raw figures show a 43% drop from an opener to a final entry,
and that is an age effect: the openers on this list are much older. Among
franchises that began in 2000 or later, final entries return 15% *more* than
openers, and taking each of those 11 franchises against itself the final entry
returns +27% on its own opener. So position in a franchise is not a discount
argument for recent titles, and anyone using it as one is reading the age of
the films. The belief that the *middle* films are the weak ones is not
supported either.

## What I recommend

**Buy franchise bundles first.** Franchise films earn back more per dollar
than standalone titles and do it more reliably, in both windows.

**Franchises, in order:** **Star Wars Collection** (6 films, typically 13.4
times back, none below 5.4 times); **The Lord of the Rings Collection** (3
films, typically 11.7 times back, none below 9.4 times); **Indiana Jones
Collection** (4 films, typically 10.9 times back, none below 4.3 times). Close
behind: Jurassic Park Collection, Back to the Future Collection, Ice Age
Collection — if a price comes back badly, substitute rather than stretch.

**Standalones, in order:** **Jaws** (1975, 67.2 times back; only this one
instalment is on offer); **The Good, the Bad and the Ugly** (1966, 32.4 times
back); **Pulp Fiction** (1994, 26.7 times back); **The Lion King** (1994, 17.0
times back; only this one instalment is on offer); **Schindler's List** (1993,
14.6 times back).

**Talent worth using as a lever.** Directors: Steven Spielberg (8 films,
typically 13.3 times back); Robert Zemeckis (4 films, typically 10.3 times
back); James Cameron (3 films, typically 9.9 times back). Leads: Mark Hamill
(3 films, typically 29.9 times back); Elijah Wood (3 films, typically 11.7
times back); Harrison Ford (5 films, typically 9.9 times back). Each carries
its film count, because a three-film record is not an eight-film record;
thinner names are in the attached review.

**The deal note.** **Star Wars Collection** is the strongest thing on the
list, and strong for a reason worth knowing before you negotiate: its value is
spread across the set, not carried by one title. Its biggest earner is 24% of
the set's total box office — the *lowest* concentration of any franchise we
looked at, and the one number that most drove this view. Elsewhere you pay
bundle prices for one title and some filler. We have no licensing quotes, so
we cannot tell you what to pay: anyone who gives you a dollar figure from this
data is guessing. Relatively, this set justifies a higher price per film than
the others, and the others more than standalones. A typical film here returns
about 158% of one in the next-best sets, so a premium of that order is
defensible; much beyond it is not.

## What I could not stand behind

In order of how much it should change your confidence.

1. **We cannot tell you how these films behave on a streaming service** — cinema takings stand in for streaming value rather than measuring it.
2. **Nothing on this list ever lost money**, so the argument that franchise films protect you on the downside cannot be tested here either way.
3. **The standalone group is thin** — 25 films, 12 since 2000, drawn from 23 franchises on the other side. Resampled, no single measure's gap is large enough for this many films to establish on its own once films in the same franchise are treated as what they are — not independent results. The direction is the more likely one on every measure, and that is the honest strength of it.
4. **Old and new money are not the same money** — old films keep earning against a fixed cost, so they look more profitable than they were. Hence two windows, never compared across.
5. **The candidate list was already filtered toward franchise films** — nearly four in five candidates belong to one, so this says which of *these* titles to buy, not what the market holds.
6. **We have no licensing prices**, so this ranks what to pursue and cannot price it.

**What would change this recommendation.** A list containing films that lost
money would let us test the safety argument properly, and could overturn it. A
wider standalone group would settle whether the gap in typical return is real,
which this many films cannot. Licensing quotes would turn a ranking into a
decision about price. None of those is a reason to wait: the ranking is the
best available on what exists today.

**The figures describe the data as it stood on 2026-11-19** and do not update
on their own. A sample of 15 titles — every exceptional performer and
everything named above — was re-checked against the source afterwards, and
every figure that matters matched.
